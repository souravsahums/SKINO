\begin{abstract}
Structure-preserving neural operators are built on a question that is rarely
stated explicitly: symplectic \emph{with respect to which inner product}? On a
non-uniform grid the discrete $L^2$ adjoint is $K^{*}=W^{-1}K^{\top}W$, where
$W$ holds the quadrature weights, and the choice of $W$ selects which
symplectic form a gradient shear preserves. We make the choice explicit and
measure it. The symplectic defect, computed from the autograd Jacobian, is exact
($4\times10^{-16}$) in the form a shear was built from and $O(1)$ in the others,
in $1$-D, $2$-D and $3$-D. Does matching the form to the grid help? On uniform
grids the matched form has the lower error in $17$ of $22$ paired comparisons
over five seeds, and none favours the other. On Chebyshev nodes, where the roles
reverse, it is better in two of three comparisons and tied in the third; on a
mapped grid where neither standard form is right it brings no consistent
benefit. Because the weights also set the kernel's quadrature, the experiments
cannot separate the form from the quadrature rule. Over $2\times10^{4}$-step
rollouts, end-to-end symplectic maps stayed bounded in $60$ of $63$ runs (the
exceptions: one mismatched-form model in $3$-D), while a lifted variant with a
non-symplectic linear lift and projection was bounded in $3$ of $12$. Bounded is
not accurate: an identity map is bounded too, the error is comparable to the
signal, and energy is off by over $100\%$, against $0.03\%$ for the reference
solver. A $783$-run benchmark places the constructions among standard
neural operators, and a worked example shows that a unit Jacobian determinant,
which every shear has, does not make an update symplectic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================================

Neural operators learn maps between function spaces and are now widely studied
as surrogates for parametric PDEs \citep{kovachki2023neural,li2021fno,lu2021deeponet}.
A fast-growing branch argues that such operators should respect the
\emph{geometric structure} of the dynamics they emulate: symplecticity for Hamiltonian systems, or the metriplectic split when dissipation is present. The
argument has a strong pedigree in finite dimensions
\citep{greydanus2019hnn,jin2020sympnets,cranmer2020lnn,zhong2020symoden}, and in
2026 several groups carried it into function space
\citep{makara2026sno,sulskis2026generic,zhang2026legonet,varghese2026pisonet}.

The function-space constructions we reviewed establish structure preservation
by construction. The Symplectic Neural Operator (SNO), for instance, defines the
symplectic form from a Hilbert inner product and notes that self-adjointness
depends on that choice, but its
self-adjoint layers are Fourier operators on the torus and its experiments use
uniform grids \citep{makara2026sno}, where the quadrature weight is a single
scalar and the weighted adjoint reduces to the transpose. On a non-uniform grid
the choice matters: the weights vary by a factor of order $N$
(Figure~\ref{fig:weights}), the adjoint is $K^{*}=W^{-1}K^{\top}W$ rather than
$K^{\top}$, and two implementations that differ in which $W$ they assume
preserve two \emph{different} symplectic forms. We are not aware of a
measurement of the symplectic defect of a trained operator against more than one
candidate inner product; this is the measurement we make.

This gives an unusually clean experiment. Build both, verify that each is exact
in its own form, then ask which one helps. We organise the study around three
questions:

\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[RQ1.] Does each construction preserve the form it claims, and only that
one? (\S\ref{sec:form})
\item[RQ2.] Does matching the form to the discretisation of the data improve
prediction, on uniform, Chebyshev and mapped grids? (\S\ref{sec:ablation})
\item[RQ3.] Does the guarantee survive the lift, projection and residual update
that conventionally wrap the core, and what does end-to-end symplecticity buy
over a long rollout? (\S\ref{sec:long})
\end{description}

The mathematics is in \S\ref{sec:background}, kept separate from the experiments
that test it; \S\ref{sec:predictions} states what it predicts for each question.
A broader nine-equation benchmark (\S\ref{sec:bench}) is secondary: it places
the constructions among standard operators, and none of the three questions
rests on it. Our contributions:

\label{sec:intro-claims}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{An instrument} (\S\ref{sec:defect}). We measure the symplectic
defect directly from the autograd Jacobian, against \emph{both} candidate inner
products, scaling to $3$-D with Hutchinson probes
\citep{hutchinson1990trace,avron2011trace} whose error we measure against the
dense value. It shows that a St\"ormer--Verlet-style update with a unit Jacobian
determinant has an $O(1)$ defect in both candidate forms
(Appendix~\ref{app:volume}).

\item \textbf{A construction} (\S\ref{sec:sacheb}, \S\ref{sec:arch}).
\sacheb{}: exact symplecticity on a non-periodic Chebyshev grid in $1$-D, $2$-D
and $3$-D, for any linear kernel and any continuously differentiable activation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb}). The gradient-shear structure follows
\citet{makara2026sno}; the weighted adjoint on non-periodic nodes is what we add.
We describe it, and the non-gradient kernel operator \ckino{} that serves as a
baseline, component by component.

\item \textbf{The form result} (RQ1 and RQ2). Each variant is exact in its own
form and $O(1)$ in the others. On uniform grids the matched form has the lower
error in $17$ of $22$ paired comparisons over five seeds ($16$ of them in every
seed) and none favours the other form; on Chebyshev nodes the preference
reverses in two of three comparisons and ties in the third; on a mapped grid
where neither standard form is the quadrature it brings no consistent benefit.
The weights also set the quadrature of the kernel integral, so the form cannot
be separated from the quadrature rule.

\item \textbf{The end-to-end result} (RQ3). Over $2\times10^{4}$ steps
end-to-end symplectic maps stayed bounded in $60$ of $63$ runs, the exceptions
being the mismatched-form model in $3$-D; a lifted variant with a
non-symplectic linear lift and projection was bounded in $3$ of $12$. Controls
temper the reading: an identity map is bounded too, an untrained symplectic map
diverges, and a $1$--$10\%$ perturbation of the adjoint is still bounded.
Boundedness tracked the spectral radius of the one-step Jacobian. Bounded is not
accurate: at that horizon the error is comparable to the signal and energy is
not conserved.

\item \textbf{A benchmark} (\S\ref{sec:setup}, \S\ref{sec:bench}): $783$ runs
over nine equations in $1$-D/$2$-D/$3$-D at matched parameter count, three
seeds, plus an unconstrained-capacity sweep showing the ranking is
budget-dependent, and $338$ further training runs for the form and
long-horizon experiments (two more seeds for the headline pairs, two further
grids, long-horizon controls and a multi-resolution test).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}
% ============================================================================

\paragraph{Neural operators.} The Fourier Neural Operator parameterises the
integral kernel by truncated Fourier multipliers \citep{li2021fno}, inheriting
discretisation invariance and $O(N\log N)$ cost; DeepONet factorises the operator
into branch and trunk networks \citep{lu2021deeponet}; the general framework and
universality results are due to \citet{kovachki2023neural}. Tensorised
\citep{kossaifi2023mgtfno} and U-Net-augmented \citep{wen2022ufno} variants
improve parameter efficiency. A practitioner-oriented account of FNO's
assumptions is given by \citet{duruisseaux2025fnoexplained}, with reference
implementations in \citet{kossaifi2024library}. Physics-informed variants add PDE
residuals to the loss \citep{raissi2019pinn,li2024pino}; these are \emph{soft}
constraints with no algebraic guarantee.

\paragraph{Structure preservation in finite dimensions.} Hamiltonian
\citep{greydanus2019hnn} and Lagrangian \citep{cranmer2020lnn} neural networks
learn the generating function; SympNets \citep{jin2020sympnets} and Symplectic
ODE-Net \citep{zhong2020symoden} parameterise symplectic maps directly. These act
on $\R^{2d}$ at a fixed discretisation and are not operators in the
function-space sense.

\paragraph{Structure-preserving neural operators.} \citet{makara2026sno}
introduce the Symplectic Neural Operator (SNO), composing symplectic shear blocks
whose update fields are gradients of learned energy functionals, realised with
self-adjoint Fourier layers on the torus, and give a modified-Hamiltonian
stability result for the learned map under stated assumptions. \citet{sulskis2026generic} embed the
GENERIC degeneracy structure \citep{grmela1997generic,ottinger2005beyond} into an
FNO, extending finite-dimensional bracket learning \citep{zhang2022gfinns}.
\citet{zhang2026legonet} compose structure-preserving blocks on boundary-adapted
spectral representations, and \citet{varghese2026pisonet} apply a conditional
symplectic operator to optimal control.

\paragraph{Where we differ.} The self-adjointness result of SNO is stated on the
torus with the Haar probability measure and its experiments use uniform grids
\citep{makara2026sno}, where $W\propto I$ and the weighted adjoint reduces to the
transpose; GENERIC-FNO is built from Fourier multipliers
\citep{sulskis2026generic}, and PI-SONet acts on latent spaces of control
problems \citep{varghese2026pisonet}. LegONet represents fields in
boundary-adapted bases and builds its structure operators from the discrete
$L^2$ metric (the mass matrix) of the basis \citep{zhang2026legonet}, but its
blocks are generator-induced vector fields in coefficient space advanced by
operator splitting, so the question we study, which weights make a nodal gradient
shear symplectic, is not posed there. Our shear field $F=K^{*}\rho(Kq)$ is the
weighted-adjoint analogue of SNO's gradient neural functional, with the
quadrature weights of the grid in place of the Haar measure.
\citet{makara2026sno} state that their formulation applies to strong canonical
symplectic Hilbert phase spaces and that weak symplectic forms and general
manifolds need additional theory; we do not address those either. We work where
the choice of weights is forced, show that it is consequential, and measure
rather than assert the outcome. Lie-equivariant lifting
\citep{finzi2020lieconv} is a standard ingredient and not claimed as a
contribution.

\subsection{The discrete inner product}
\label{sec:ip}

Let $\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}=[-1,1]$ be discretised on the $N+1$ CGL nodes
$x_j=\cos(j\pi/N)$. Functions are represented by nodal values, and the physical
$L^2$ product is approximated by Clenshaw--Curtis quadrature
\citep{clenshaw1960,trefethen2000spectral},
\begin{equation}
\iprod{f}{g}_W \;=\; \int_{\Omega_{\mathrm{d}}} f g\,dx \;\approx\; f^{\top} W g,
\qquad W=\mathrm{diag}(w_0,\dots,w_N),\quad w_j>0 .
\label{eq:ip}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/weights.png}
\caption{The geometric fact the paper rests on. \textbf{(a)} CGL nodes cluster
quadratically at the boundary; a uniform grid does not. \textbf{(b)} The
Clenshaw--Curtis weights therefore vary by $78\times$ across the domain at
$N=24$, so $W\neq cI$ and consequently $K^{*}\neq K^{\top}$. On a uniform grid
the two coincide, which is why a Fourier-parameterised operator using the
standard uniform quadrature does not meet the distinction. \textbf{(c)} The
Chebyshev basis assumes no periodicity at the domain ends.}
\label{fig:weights}
\end{figure}

Phase space is $\mathbb{P}=\mathcal{H}_q\oplus\mathcal{H}_p$ with
$\mathcal{H}=(\R^{N+1},\iprod{\cdot}{\cdot}_W)$, carrying
\begin{equation}
\omega_W(z,z') = \iprod{q}{p'}_W - \iprod{p}{q'}_W = z^{\top}\Omega z',
\qquad
\Omega=\begin{pmatrix} 0 & W \\ -W & 0\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:omega}
\end{equation}
This is the finite-dimensional shadow of the weak symplectic form on a Hilbert
phase space \citep{weinstein1969symplectic,marsden1999mechanics}; the point of
what follows is that the discretisation chooses $W$, and that choice is not
innocuous.

\begin{definition}[Weighted adjoint]
The adjoint of $K$ with respect to \eqref{eq:ip} is the unique $K^{*}$ with
$\iprod{Kf}{g}_W=\iprod{f}{K^{*}g}_W$ for all $f,g$. Expanding,
$f^{\top}K^{\top}Wg=f^{\top}WK^{*}g$, hence
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;K^{*}=W^{-1}K^{\top}W\;}
\label{eq:adjoint}
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

\subsection{When is a shear symplectic?}
\label{sec:criterion}

\begin{proposition}[Shear criterion]
\label{prop:shear}
Let $\Phi(q,p)=(q,\,p+F(q))$ with $A=DF(q)$. Then
\begin{equation}
(D\Phi)^{\top}\Omega\,(D\Phi)-\Omega
=\begin{pmatrix} WA - A^{\top}W & 0\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:defect-identity}
\end{equation}
so $\Phi$ preserves $\omega_W$ \emph{iff} $A$ is self-adjoint in $W$, i.e.\
$WA=A^{\top}W$. The transposed shear behaves analogously.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$D\Phi=\begin{psmallmatrix} I&0\\ A&I\end{psmallmatrix}$, so
$\Omega D\Phi=\begin{psmallmatrix} WA& W\\ -W&0\end{psmallmatrix}$ and
$(D\Phi)^{\top}\Omega D\Phi
=\begin{psmallmatrix} I&A^{\top}\\ 0&I\end{psmallmatrix}
 \begin{psmallmatrix} WA& W\\ -W&0\end{psmallmatrix}
=\begin{psmallmatrix} WA-A^{\top}W& W\\ -W&0\end{psmallmatrix}$.
Subtract $\Omega$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Volume preservation is not symplecticity]
\label{rem:volume}
$\det D\Phi=1$ for \emph{every} shear, whatever $A$. Unit Jacobian determinant is
Liouville volume preservation \citep{arnold1989mechanics}, strictly weaker than
preservation of $\omega_W$,
which by Proposition~\ref{prop:shear} additionally requires $WA=A^{\top}W$.
Conflating the two is an easy way for an architecture described as symplectic to
fall short of the claim; \S\ref{sec:form} and Appendix~\ref{app:volume} give an
instance, a St\"ormer--Verlet-style block with a unit-triangular Jacobian whose
measured defect is $O(1)$ in both candidate forms.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The question is under-specified]
\label{rem:illposed}
Because \eqref{eq:defect-identity} depends on $W$, ``is this block symplectic?''
has no answer until $W$ is named. Two shears built with the adjoint taken in
different $W$ are \emph{both} exactly symplectic, in different forms. This is
the observation the rest of the paper develops. (In our implementation $W$
also enters the kernel $K$ itself, as its quadrature; the theorem holds for any
$K$, so this does not affect it, but it matters for what the experiments can
separate, \S\ref{sec:sacheb-arch}.)
\end{remark}

\subsection{A gradient shear that is exact in a chosen form}
\label{sec:sacheb}

The construction below makes the self-adjointness required by
Proposition~\ref{prop:shear} hold by design. The state $q$ carries $c$ channels
on the $(N+1)^d$ grid nodes, $q\in\R^{c\times(N+1)^d}$. Let
$K:\R^{c\times(N+1)^d}\to\R^{h\times(N+1)^d}$ be linear, that is, a matrix
acting on the flattened channel--node index; the low-rank Chebyshev kernel used
in practice is one such matrix (\S\ref{sec:sacheb-arch}). The weight acts
channel by channel, $W_{\mathrm{in}}=I_c\otimes W$ on the domain and
$W_{\mathrm{out}}=I_h\otimes W$ on the range, both diagonal and positive, with
$W=\bigotimes_{a=1}^{d}W_a$ in $d$ dimensions. The weighted adjoint is then
$K^{*}=W_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}K^{\top}W_{\mathrm{out}}$, which is
\eqref{eq:adjoint} when $c=h=1$, and the phase-space form is
$\omega_{W_{\mathrm{in}}}$. Let $R\in C^2(\R)$ with $\rho=R'$, both applied
entrywise, and define the energy functional and its gradient in
$\iprod{\cdot}{\cdot}_{W_{\mathrm{in}}}$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}(q)=\mathbf{1}^{\top}W_{\mathrm{out}}\,R(Kq)
\;\approx\;\sum_{\text{channels}}\int_\Omega R\big((Kq)(x)\big)\,dx,
\qquad
F(q) \;=\; \nabla_{W_{\mathrm{in}}}\mathcal{E}(q) \;=\; K^{*}\rho(Kq).
\label{eq:field}
\end{equation}
The block alternates
\begin{equation}
\Phi^{\uparrow}(q,p)=(q,\;p+F_\theta(q)),
\qquad
\Phi^{\downarrow}(q,p)=(q+G_\vartheta(p),\;p).
\label{eq:block}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Exact symplecticity, any dimension]
\label{thm:sacheb}
Let $K$, $W_{\mathrm{in}}$, $W_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $R$ be as above. For any
entries of $K$, any $R\in C^2$ (equivalently $\rho\in C^1$), any resolution $N$,
any channel counts $c,h$ and any spatial dimension $d\in\{1,2,3\}$, the map
$\Phi^{\uparrow}$ of \eqref{eq:block} satisfies
$(\Phi^{\uparrow})^{*}\omega_{W_{\mathrm{in}}}=\omega_{W_{\mathrm{in}}}$ exactly,
and likewise $\Phi^{\downarrow}$; hence so does any finite alternating
composition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $D=\mathrm{diag}\big(\rho'((Kq)_k)\big)$, diagonal over the channel--node
index $k$ of the range, so $A=DF=K^{*}DK$. Since
$(K^{*})^{\top}=W_{\mathrm{out}}KW_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}$,
\begin{align*}
W_{\mathrm{in}}A &= W_{\mathrm{in}}\big(W_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}K^{\top}W_{\mathrm{out}}\big)DK
= K^{\top}W_{\mathrm{out}}DK,\\
A^{\top}W_{\mathrm{in}} &= K^{\top}D\,W_{\mathrm{out}}KW_{\mathrm{in}}^{-1}W_{\mathrm{in}}
= K^{\top}DW_{\mathrm{out}}K .
\end{align*}
$W_{\mathrm{out}}$ and $D$ are both diagonal on the range and therefore commute,
so $W_{\mathrm{in}}A=A^{\top}W_{\mathrm{in}}$; apply
Proposition~\ref{prop:shear} with $W_{\mathrm{in}}$ in place of $W$. The
dimension $d$ and the channel counts enter only through the sizes of these
diagonal matrices. For $\Phi^{\downarrow}$ exchange the roles of $q$ and $p$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Regularity of the activation]
\label{rem:activation}
The proof uses $\rho'$ only through $D$, so it needs $\rho$ to be differentiable
at the points $(Kq)_k$. Our implementation uses $\rho=\mathrm{GELU}$ \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}, which is
$C^\infty$, so the theorem holds at every state. For a piecewise-$C^1$
activation such as ReLU, $\rho'$ fails to exist only where some $(Kq)_k$ sits
at the kink, a finite union of hyperplanes in $q$. Away from that set the
identity $W_{\mathrm{in}}A=A^{\top}W_{\mathrm{in}}$ holds as stated, so the
shear is symplectic almost everywhere; on it the Jacobian is undefined.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} says the block is symplectic in \emph{the form its
adjoint was built from}. It says nothing about any other form, and the next
result shows that the omission is not a technicality: it is the whole content of
the paper, and it predicts the pattern that RQ1 tests (\S\ref{sec:form}).

\begin{proposition}[Which form, and only which form]
\label{prop:necessity}
Fix a positive diagonal $W_1$ and build the shear field with the adjoint taken in
$W_1$, i.e.\ $F(q)=K^{*}_{W_1}\rho(Kq)$ with $K^{*}_{W_1}=W_1^{-1}K^{\top}W_1$,
so that $A=K^{*}_{W_1}DK$. Let $W_2$ be any other positive diagonal weight. Then
\begin{equation}
W_2A - A^{\top}W_2 \;=\; [\,S,\,M\,] \;=\; SM-MS,
\qquad S := W_2W_1^{-1},\quad M := K^{\top}W_1DK ,
\label{eq:commutator}
\end{equation}
where $S$ is diagonal and $M$ is symmetric. Consequently
\begin{equation}
\big(W_2A-A^{\top}W_2\big)_{ij} = (s_i-s_j)\,M_{ij},
\label{eq:entrywise}
\end{equation}
and the shear is symplectic in $\omega_{W_2}$ \emph{for every} $K$ and $\rho$ if
and only if $W_2\propto W_1$. We state this for $c=h=1$; with channels, each
$W_i$ becomes $I\otimes W_i$ on the domain and on the range, and the proof is
unchanged.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $W_1$ is symmetric, $(K^{*}_{W_1})^{\top}=(W_1^{-1}K^{\top}W_1)^{\top}
=W_1KW_1^{-1}$. Diagonal matrices commute, so $W_1D=DW_1$ and
$W_1^{-1}W_2=W_2W_1^{-1}=S$. Then
\[
W_2A = W_2W_1^{-1}K^{\top}W_1DK = S\,\big(K^{\top}W_1DK\big) = SM ,
\]
\[
A^{\top}W_2 = K^{\top}D\,(K^{*}_{W_1})^{\top}W_2
            = K^{\top}D\,W_1KW_1^{-1}W_2
            = \big(K^{\top}W_1DK\big)S = MS ,
\]
which gives \eqref{eq:commutator}. $M$ is symmetric because
$M^{\top}=K^{\top}(W_1D)^{\top}K=K^{\top}DW_1K=K^{\top}W_1DK=M$. Writing
$S=\mathrm{diag}(s_i)$ gives $(SM-MS)_{ij}=s_iM_{ij}-M_{ij}s_j$, which is
\eqref{eq:entrywise}.

For the equivalence: if $W_2=cW_1$ then $S=cI$, every $s_i-s_j$ vanishes and so does the defect, recovering Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} at $c=1$. Conversely,
suppose $W_2\not\propto W_1$, so there are indices $i\neq j$ with $s_i\neq s_j$.
Choose $D=I$ (take $\rho'\equiv1$) and $K=e_ie_i^{\top}+e_je_j^{\top}$; then
$M=K^{\top}W_1K$ has $M_{ij}=0$, so pick instead
$K=(e_i+e_j)e_i^{\top}+(e_i+e_j)e_j^{\top}$, for which
$M_{ij}=(w_{1,i}+w_{1,j})\neq0$. By \eqref{eq:entrywise} the $(i,j)$ entry of the
defect is $(s_i-s_j)M_{ij}\neq0$, so the shear is not symplectic in
$\omega_{W_2}$ for that parameter choice.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The defect scales with the spread of $W_2/W_1$]
\label{rem:spread}
Equation~\eqref{eq:entrywise} says the mismatch is governed by the
\emph{spread} of the ratio $s_i=w_{2,i}/w_{1,i}$: for a fixed $M$ every entry of
the defect scales with a difference $s_i-s_j$, so a wider dynamic range in
$W_2W_1^{-1}$ should produce a larger defect. This is a heuristic rather than a
bound, because the normalisation of the defect in \eqref{eq:defect} below also
depends on $W_2$, but it is falsifiable. For $\Wc$ against $\Wu$ the per-axis
ratio spans a factor of $\approx\pi N$, since the Clenshaw--Curtis weights fall
from $\approx\pi/N$ in the interior to $O(N^{-2})$ at the ends, and the
tensor-product weight $\bigotimes_a W_a$ compounds this to $(\pi N)^d$. The
off-diagonal defect should therefore grow with dimension; RQ1 tests this
(\S\ref{sec:form}).
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Low-rank realisation.} For the low-rank kernel and its weighted
adjoint (Appendix~\ref{app:lowrank}),
\[
Kv=\sum_r\varphi_r M_r\iprod{\psi_r}{v}_W,
\qquad
K^{*}g=\sum_r\psi_r M_r^{\top}\iprod{\varphi_r}{g}_W :
\]
\emph{swap $\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi$ and transpose $M$}. No inverse of $W$ is
ever formed and the cost stays $O(RN^d)$.

\subsection{Measuring the defect}
\label{sec:defect}

Proposition~\ref{prop:shear} turns the question into a number. For a shear field
$F$ we form $A=DF$ by reverse-mode differentiation and report
\begin{equation}
\defect_W(F) \;=\; \frac{\lVert WA-A^{\top}W\rVert_F}{\lVert WA\rVert_F},
\label{eq:defect}
\end{equation}
zero iff the block preserves $\omega_W$. Crucially we evaluate
\eqref{eq:defect} for \emph{both} $W\in\{\Wc,\Wu\}$: reporting one column can hide the
matched form altogether, since a map that is exactly symplectic in $\Wu$ has an
$O(1)$ defect when scored only against $\Wc$.

\paragraph{Scaling to 3-D.} A dense Jacobian costs one backward pass per degree
of freedom: $65$ passes for a $1$-D grid at $N=64$, but $17^3=4913$ at $N=16$
in $3$-D, for every model, form and seed.
Proposition~\ref{prop:hutch} replaces it with a probe estimator, an instance of
Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator
\citep{hutchinson1990trace,avron2011trace}, whose \emph{number} of
automatic-differentiation passes does not grow with the grid.

\begin{proposition}[Probe estimators for the squared norms]
\label{prop:hutch}
Let $M_\ast := WA-A^{\top}W$ and let $v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. Then
\begin{equation}
M_\ast v \;=\; W(Av)\;-\;A^{\top}(Wv),
\qquad
\mathbb{E}_v\big[\lVert M_\ast v\rVert^2\big] \;=\; \lVert M_\ast\rVert_F^2 ,
\label{eq:hutch}
\end{equation}
so averaging $\lVert M_\ast v\rVert^2$ over $m$ independent probes is an unbiased
estimator of $\lVert M_\ast\rVert_F^2$, and the same argument applied to $WA$ is
unbiased for $\lVert WA\rVert_F^2$. Each probe costs one Jacobian--vector product
($Av$) and one vector--Jacobian product ($A^{\top}u$), so the \emph{number} of
differentiation passes is $2m$ whatever the state dimension.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first identity is linearity: $(WA-A^{\top}W)v=W(Av)-A^{\top}(Wv)$, and the two
terms are exactly a JVP and a VJP of the shear field, so neither requires $A$ to
be formed. For the second, $\mathbb{E}[vv^{\top}]=I$ gives
\[
\mathbb{E}_v\big[\lVert M_\ast v\rVert^2\big]
= \mathbb{E}_v\big[v^{\top}M_\ast^{\top}M_\ast v\big]
= \mathrm{tr}\big(M_\ast^{\top}M_\ast\,\mathbb{E}[vv^{\top}]\big)
= \mathrm{tr}\big(M_\ast^{\top}M_\ast\big)
= \lVert M_\ast\rVert_F^2 .
\]
The same argument applied to $WA$ estimates the denominator of
\eqref{eq:defect}, so the ratio is formed from two estimates sharing the JVP.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is unbiased, and what the estimator costs]
\label{rem:ratio}
Proposition~\ref{prop:hutch} is unbiased for the two \emph{squared} norms, not
for their ratio. What we report is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\defect}_W
=\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lVert M_\ast v_i\rVert^2}
            {\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lVert WAv_i\rVert^2}\right)^{1/2},
\label{eq:ratio-est}
\end{equation}
a ratio of square roots of two unbiased estimates. This is consistent as
$m\to\infty$ but is not itself unbiased, and we do not claim otherwise.
Numerator and denominator share probes, so their fluctuations are correlated
and partly cancel in the ratio; \S\ref{sec:form} measures how much. Two
accounting points are worth stating plainly, because it would be easy
to overclaim both. First, what is independent of the grid is the \emph{number}
of differentiation passes, $2m$ rather than one per degree of freedom; the
runtime of each pass still scales with the cost of evaluating the operator,
$O(RN^d)$ for the low-rank kernel of \S\ref{sec:sacheb}. The saving is a factor
of $N^d/2m$ in passes, not a constant-time measurement. Second, the estimator is
stochastic, so an $O(1)$ off-diagonal value carries probe noise, which
\S\ref{sec:form} quantifies against the dense value; a genuinely symplectic
block returns zero for every probe, so the machine-precision diagonal carries
none.
\end{remark}

\subsection{What the theory predicts, and what it leaves open}
\label{sec:predictions}

The results above fix one expectation for each research question of
\S\ref{sec:intro}, and leave open the matter the experiments exist to settle.

\textbf{P1, for RQ1.} A gradient shear built with the adjoint in $W_1$ has zero
defect in $\omega_{W_1}$ at every resolution and dimension
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb}), and non-zero defect in any form not proportional to
it (Proposition~\ref{prop:necessity}), growing with the spread of $W_2W_1^{-1}$
and hence with dimension (Remark~\ref{rem:spread}). \ckino{}'s block has no
self-adjoint structure and should be non-zero in both forms
(Remark~\ref{rem:volume}).

\textbf{P2, for RQ2.} Nothing above says which form helps prediction. Both
variants are exactly symplectic; the theory says only that they preserve
different forms. The hypothesis RQ2 tests, that the form whose weights are the
quadrature of the data's own grid is the one that helps, is a hypothesis about
learning, not a consequence of the theorem.

\textbf{P3, for RQ3.} Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} covers the shears and their
compositions. It says nothing about a map that also contains a lift, a
projection or a residual update that is not symplectic. Nor does symplecticity
imply boundedness under iteration even where it holds: the linear map
$(q,p)\mapsto(\lambda q,\lambda^{-1}p)$ with $\lambda>1$ is symplectic in every
$\omega_W$ and unbounded. Whether end-to-end symplecticity goes with bounded long
rollouts is therefore an empirical question, and RQ3 treats it as one.

\paragraph{(i) Lie-equivariant lifting.} A pointwise or short-stencil map raising
the $C$ physical fields to $H$ hidden channels, built from generators of a small
Lie group so that the lift commutes with translations of the input
\citep{finzi2020lieconv}. Two variants matter later: a \emph{convolutional} lift
(resolution-dependent stencil) and a \emph{spectral} lift (FFT-derivative, exactly
resolution-invariant). \S\ref{sec:support} shows these trade invariance against
accuracy.

\paragraph{(ii) Separable Chebyshev kernel integral.} The operator core, a
low-rank separable kernel in the spirit of Mercer's expansion
\citep{mercer1909},
\begin{equation}
(Kv)(x)=\sum_{r=1}^{R}\Big(\textstyle\prod_{a=1}^{d}\varphi^a_r(x_a)\Big)\,
        \sigma_r M_r \iprod{\textstyle\prod_a\psi^a_r}{v}_W ,
\label{eq:ckino-kernel}
\end{equation}
with $\varphi^a_r,\psi^a_r$ stored as $n_{\mathrm{train}}{+}1$ Chebyshev
coefficients per axis, $M_r\in\R^{H\times H}$ mixing channels and $\sigma_r>0$ a
learned Mercer scale. Cost is $O(RN^d)$ rather than $O(N^{2d})$, and because the
basis functions are polynomials the same weights evaluate on any grid.

\paragraph{(iii) St\"ormer--Verlet-style block.} Hidden channels are split into
$(q,p)$ halves and updated by alternating half-steps with a learned step size,
in the manner of the St\"ormer--Verlet scheme \citep{verlet1967,hairer2006gni}.
The Jacobian of each half-step is unit triangular, so the block is
volume-preserving, but it is not symplectic in any of the forms we consider:
\eqref{eq:ckino-kernel} uses \emph{independent} $\varphi_r$ and $\psi_r$ and an
unconstrained $M_r$, so nothing forces $k(x,y)=k(y,x)^{\top}$ and the Jacobian is
not self-adjoint (Appendix~\ref{app:volume}).

\paragraph{(iv) Projection.} A pointwise map back to $C$ output channels.

\subsection{\sacheb{} as an architecture}
\label{sec:sacheb-arch}

\sacheb{} keeps the separable low-rank Chebyshev kernel of \ckino{} (ii) and
replaces the St\"ormer--Verlet-style block (iii) with the gradient shears of
\S\ref{sec:sacheb}. Each shear field is $F=K^{*}\rho(Kq)$
(Figure~\ref{fig:sacheb}), where $K$ is the low-rank kernel, mapping the $c$
channels of its argument to $h=\max(4,R)$ hidden channels in which the potential
acts; $K^{*}$ is its weighted adjoint in the low-rank form of
Appendix~\ref{app:lowrank}; and $\rho$ is the GELU \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}. Shears alternate between
updating $p$ from $q$ and $q$ from $p$, $2\ell$ of them at depth $\ell$, each
scaled by a learned gain initialised at zero so that every shear starts as the
identity map.
Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} therefore applies to every block. The lifted variants
add a pointwise linear lift and projection (a $1\times1$ convolution) and a
residual update; the lift-free variants add nothing (\S\ref{sec:lift}). The two
forms differ in the weight $W$, and $W$ enters twice: as the quadrature of the
kernel integral inside $K$, $Kv=\sum_r\varphi_rM_r\iprod{\psi_r}{v}_W$, and,
through the same inner product, in $K^{*}$. With identical parameters the two
forms therefore give different forward maps (a $26\%$ relative difference on a
random input at $N=16$), while $K^{*}=W^{-1}K^{\top}W$ holds to $10^{-16}$ in
each. Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} holds for every $K$, so it is unaffected; what
changes is what the experiments can separate. Changing the form also changes the
quadrature rule of the kernel integral, so a model that does better with one
form may owe it to either, and we cannot tell which (\S\ref{sec:limits}). A
control that decouples them, with the kernel quadrature in one weight and the
adjoint taken in the other, is straightforward to build; we have not run it.

\subsection{Lifted versus lift-free, and why it matters}
\label{sec:lift}

Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} is a statement about the \emph{block}. A deployed
operator usually wraps blocks in a pointwise lift and projection and is driven
residually, $x_{t+1}=x_t+\mathcal{N}(x_t)$. None of those three maps is
symplectic in general, so the composition carries no guarantee
(Figure~\ref{fig:lift}). We therefore build both:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Lifted} (\texttt{sacheb}, \texttt{sacheb\_naive}): $H\gg C$ hidden
channels, maximal capacity, symplectic \emph{core} only.
\item \textbf{Lift-free} (\texttt{sacheb\_pure}, \texttt{sacheb\_pure\_naive}):
the shears act directly on the PDE's own $(q,p)$ fields, $H=C$, no lift, no
projection, non-residual update. The \emph{deployed map} is exactly symplectic;
capacity must come from rank and depth instead. This requires a canonical
$(q,p)$ pair and so applies only to two-field problems.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What each one tests.} \texttt{advection} is pure transport: linear,
non-dispersive, and conserving both $\int u$ and $\int u^2$, so it is the floor
below which a surrogate has no excuse. \texttt{heat} is linear but strictly
dissipative, which makes it a control: energy decay is the correct behaviour, so
a model that artificially conserves energy is penalised rather than rewarded.
\texttt{burgers} adds nonlinearity to dissipation \citep{burgers1948} and steepens
into shock fronts, testing whether a spectral parameterisation can represent a
sharp front without Gibbs oscillation \citep{gottlieb1997gibbs}. \texttt{kdv} was
intended as the nonlinear dispersive case, the Korteweg--de Vries equation
\citep{korteweg1895}, a Hamiltonian PDE under the non-canonical bracket of
\citet{gardner1971kdv}. As generated it is the linear dispersive equation
$u_t=u_{xxx}$ (footnote $\dagger$ of Table~\ref{tab:systems}), a conservative
problem with the cubic dispersion relation $\omega=k^{3}$, so that components of
different wavelength separate, and with no nonlinear interaction. We keep the
identifier \texttt{kdv} and report it as the linear dispersive problem; the stiff
$u_{xxx}$ term is the reason for $\Delta t=10^{-3}$ and ten micro-steps.
\texttt{ns2d} is decaying two-dimensional turbulence in vorticity form, the
canonical neural-operator benchmark, and the only problem here with a genuine
energy cascade.

The four wave problems carry the conserved Hamiltonian
$\mathcal{H}=\tfrac12\int(v^2+c^2\lVert\nabla u\rVert^2)$ and are integrated by
St\"ormer--Verlet leap-frog \citep{verlet1967,hairer2006gni}, so the reference
trajectories are themselves symplectic. This matters for interpreting \S\ref{sec:long}: when we ask whether
a learned operator conserves energy over $2\times10^{4}$ steps, the ground truth
against which it is compared does so by construction.

\texttt{wave1d\_dir} is the discriminating rung. It is the wave equation on
$[0,L]$ with homogeneous \emph{Dirichlet} boundaries, a uniform grid, centred
differences and symplectic leap-frog, with initial conditions formed from a
truncated Chebyshev series times the envelope $x(L-x)$. The boundary conditions,
grid and initial-condition family follow the wave experiment of
\citet{makara2026sno}; the wave speed ($c=1$ against $0.05$), the integrator
(leap-frog against RK45), the amount of training data and the model size
($\approx25$k parameters against about $2.3$M) differ, so our SNO results are not
a reproduction of theirs. The problem is simultaneously Hamiltonian, so
symplecticity is meaningful, and non-periodic, so a Fourier parameterisation is
structurally mismatched. The reference solver conserves energy to
$2.3\times10^{-4}$ over $500$ steps and holds the boundary at exactly zero.

\paragraph{Two wave problems on non-uniform nodes.} \texttt{wave1d\_cgl} is the
Dirichlet wave equation on $[-1,1]$ sampled at $65$ Chebyshev--Gauss--Lobatto
(CGL) nodes, where Clenshaw--Curtis is the correct quadrature and $\Wc$ is the
matched form. With the first-derivative matrix $D$ on the nodes and the
Clenshaw--Curtis weights $W$, the semi-discrete operator $A=-W^{-1}D^{\top}WD$
satisfies $WA=A^{\top}W$, so $u_t=v$, $v_t=c^2Au$ is canonically Hamiltonian in
$\omega_W$ with $H=\tfrac12\big(v^{\top}Wv+c^2(Du)^{\top}W(Du)\big)$. The
reference trajectories are St\"ormer--Verlet on the interior nodes with the
boundary held at zero; they are symplectic in that form ($7\times10^{-17}$ in the
relative defect) and $H$ changes by $3\times10^{-5}$ over $500$ stored steps. The
stored step is $0.01$, giving $c\,\Delta t/L=0.005$ as for \texttt{wave1d\_dir}.
\texttt{wave1d\_kte} is the same equation on the Kosloff--Tal-Ezer grid
\citep{kosloff1993}, $x_j=\arcsin(\alpha\xi_j)/\arcsin\alpha$ with $\alpha=0.995$
and $\xi_j$ the CGL nodes, which relaxes the clustering of the CGL nodes and the
time-step restriction that comes with it. Its quadrature is the Clenshaw--Curtis
weight times $dx/d\xi$, which is neither constant nor Clenshaw--Curtis, so both
standard forms are mismatched there and only a third form $W_{\mathrm{kte}}$ is
matched.

The table answers the questions a reader needs settled before the ablation can
be read.

\emph{Is anything resampled?} No. Each uniform sample is handed to the model as
the value at the corresponding CGL node, without interpolation, and both
variants receive identical inputs.

\emph{What does the operator then represent?} Because
$T_k(\cos\theta)=\cos k\theta$, the Chebyshev basis evaluated at node $j$ is
$T_k(x_j)=\cos(k\pi j/N)$. In the physical coordinate $s_j=j/N$ of the uniform
grid this is $\cos(k\pi s_j)$, so on these data the operator's kernel functions
are cosine series on a uniform grid. The parameterisation never assumes
periodicity, which is what matters for the boundary results of
\S\ref{sec:basis}, but the operator is not acting on data sampled at Chebyshev
nodes.

\emph{Which inner product is the physical one?} The solver's quadrature,
$\iprod{f}{g}\approx h\sum_jf_jg_j$, which is proportional to $\Wu$: exactly so
on the periodic grids, and on every non-zero entry for \texttt{wave1d\_dir},
whose boundary values vanish. A constant rescaling of $W$ does not change which
maps preserve the form (Proposition~\ref{prop:necessity} with $W_2=cW_1$), so
$\Wu$ is the matched form.

\emph{What, then, does $\Wc$ do?} It attaches the Clenshaw--Curtis weights,
approximately $\frac{\pi}{N}\sin(\pi s_j)$, to uniformly spaced samples. That is
a legitimate inner product, the quadrature of $\int fg\,\pi\sin(\pi s)\,ds$, but
not the physical one: it weights the middle of the domain up to $\approx\pi N$
times more than the ends.

\emph{Where do the two forms enter?} In the quadrature $\iprod{\psi_r}{v}_W$ of the
kernel integral and, through the same inner product, in the adjoint
$K^{*}=W^{-1}K^{\top}W$ of \eqref{eq:adjoint} (\S\ref{sec:sacheb-arch}). The basis,
rank, channel mixing, initialisation and inputs are identical.

The consequence is worth stating, since it bounds what the nine-problem
experiments can establish. Because every one of the nine matrix grids is uniform, $\Wu$ is
the matched form throughout and $\Wc$ the mismatched one throughout, so they
test the matching hypothesis in one direction only: they cannot by themselves
separate ``matching the form to the grid is what matters'' from ``constant
weights are simply the better choice''. The two further Hamiltonian problems
of Table~\ref{tab:systems}, one sampled on CGL nodes where $\Wc$ is the matched
form and one on a mapped grid where neither standard form is, test the other
directions (\S\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Data, splits and selection}
\label{sec:splits}

Trajectories are generated from random initial conditions with \emph{disjoint
seed ranges} per split, so no initial condition appears in more than one split.
Each $1$-D problem uses $512$ training trajectories of $600$ steps and is trained
for $15$ epochs; \texttt{wave2d} and \texttt{ns2d} use $96$ trajectories of $300$
steps ($15$ epochs), and \texttt{wave3d} uses $48$ trajectories of $200$ steps
($12$ epochs). In the main matrix every problem has $12$ validation and $12$ test
trajectories; the additional runs of \S\ref{sec:extras} use $32$ test
trajectories.
The data seeds are fixed independently of the training seed, so every model and
every seed is scored on the \emph{same} test trajectories; the training seeds vary
only initialisation and minibatch order. Rollout metrics are always computed on
the test split, and are evaluated once.

\paragraph{Training.} All models are trained with AdamW
\citep{loshchilov2019adamw}, a variant of Adam \citep{kingma2015adam} with
decoupled weight decay ($10^{-5}$), at a peak learning rate of $3\times10^{-3}$
annealed by a cosine schedule \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr} to $3\times10^{-5}$, with
gradient-norm clipping at $1$ and batches of $32$. Recursive models are trained
by push-forward unrolling over horizons of $1$, $2$ and $4$ steps in equal thirds
of the epochs, with teacher forcing decaying from $1$ to $0$ and, in the noisy
configurations, input noise of relative size $2\%$; non-recursive \texttt{seq2seq}
models predict a window of future states from the initial state in one pass, with
the number of gradient steps matched to the recursive models. Everything is implemented
in PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}; the baselines are our own implementations
from the published descriptions. The long-horizon and multi-resolution runs use the
same optimiser, schedule and unrolling curriculum, without input noise, and $12$
epochs.

One caveat applies to Table~\ref{tab:winners} specifically. The best
configuration per problem is the one with the lowest mean over three seeds of
test-split relative RMS at rollout step $200$ ($150$ in $3$-D), compared across
all configurations; validation is not used for this selection. Selecting the
minimum of many noisy scores biases it optimistically
\citep{cawley2010overfitting}, so the per-problem
winners should be read as an upper estimate rather than an unbiased one. The
comparisons the three research questions rest on are not exposed to this: the
defect matrix (\S\ref{sec:form}), the form ablation (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) and
the long-horizon rollouts (\S\ref{sec:long}) are all paired
comparisons between variants that differ in one design decision (the weight $W$,
or the presence of the lift), with no selection over a pool.

\subsection{Design, matching and cost}
\label{sec:design}

Families are crossed with prediction mode
$\{$recursive, non-recursive \texttt{seq2seq}$\}$, training noise
$\{$none, $2\%\}$ and an optional PDE-residual loss, giving $33$ configurations
in $1$-D and $21$ in $2$-D/$3$-D, all matched to $\approx$25k parameters. Three
seeds on a single accelerator type (NVIDIA Tesla T4): \textbf{783 training runs}.
The additional runs of \S\ref{sec:extras} add $338$.

Parameter count is the matching criterion, and it is the weakest of the
available ones. The families compared here have very different arithmetic
profiles at equal parameter count: a Fourier layer spends its budget on
multipliers applied through an FFT of cost $O(N^d\log N)$, a low-rank integral
kernel on $O(RN^d)$ contractions, attention on a quadratic term in sequence
length, and a U-Net on convolutions across a resolution hierarchy. Autoregressive
rollout multiplies all of these by the number of steps, while the
\texttt{seq2seq} variants pay once. Equal parameters therefore does not mean
equal memory, equal FLOPs or equal wall-clock, and none of the accuracy
comparisons in \S\ref{sec:accuracy} should be read as statements about
efficiency. Measured throughput is reported separately in \S\ref{sec:support},
where the spread is roughly an order of magnitude.

\paragraph{Metrics.} Relative RMS at rollout checkpoints, plus amplitude ratio,
pattern correlation, spectral error and invariant drift, collapsed into a
\emph{verdict} and a \emph{usable horizon} (last step with correlation $\ge0.9$
and amplitude ratio in $[0.7,1.4]$). A model can post a middling RMS while being
physically dead, and only the decomposition separates the two. For the
long-horizon rollouts of \S\ref{sec:long} we report four of these separately,
each averaged over the $8$ test trajectories of those runs (\S\ref{sec:extras}):
relative RMS against the
reference, the measure of \emph{trajectory} error; pattern correlation, the
Pearson correlation of the mean-removed predicted and reference fields, which
measures \emph{phase}; amplitude ratio, the ratio of their standard deviations;
and energy drift $|E(t)-E(0)|/E(0)$, computed with the solver's own energy
functional on the model's own trajectory.

\subsection{Additional seeds, grids and controls}
\label{sec:extras}

Further experiments extend the main matrix in four ways and address the
limitations stated in \S\ref{sec:nodes} and \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\paragraph{Two further grids.} The wave problems \texttt{wave1d\_cgl} and
\texttt{wave1d\_kte} of \S\ref{sec:systems} test the matching hypothesis in the
other directions: on CGL nodes $\Wc$ is the matched form, and on the mapped grid
neither standard form is. As before, the operator reads sample $j$ as the CGL
node $\xi_j$.

\paragraph{Seeds, batches and uncertainty.} The lifted and lift-free pairs of
the nine uniform problems were run with five seeds ($0$--$4$); \texttt{wave1d\_cgl}
and \texttt{wave1d\_kte} used five throughout. Seeds $0$--$2$ belong to the main
matrix, which ran on PyTorch 2.8 and scored $12$ test trajectories per run; seeds
$3$--$4$ and the two further grids ran on PyTorch 2.13 and scored $32$ test
trajectories per run, from the same split seeds. The two batches therefore differ
in software and in test-set size, and we pool them only through the per-seed
error. For each pair we take, per seed, the ratio of the mismatched to the matched
model's relative RMS at step $200$ ($150$ in $3$-D), with errors above $10^{3}$
clipped as in \S\ref{sec:splits}, and report the number of seeds won and the
median ratio with its range. A comparison is called only if a $95\%$ percentile
bootstrap interval \citep{efron1994bootstrap} for the mean log-ratio over seeds
excludes zero \emph{and} the median ratio differs from one by at least $5\%$;
otherwise it is reported as inconclusive. The $5\%$ floor is an analysis choice
made after inspecting the data, not a pre-specified one: it was introduced when a
$1\%$ difference was flagged by an interval whose lower end was barely above one
while three of five seeds agreed. It can only turn a call into an inconclusive
one, and it changes none of the uniform-grid or CGL calls. The intervals are
descriptive: with five seeds they are not a substitute for a formal test.

\paragraph{Long-horizon protocol and controls.} Each long-horizon model is
trained on $384$ trajectories of $400$ steps for $12$ epochs with a recursive
curriculum, then rolled out for $2\times10^{4}$ steps from the first $8$ test
initial conditions, scoring at log-spaced checkpoints; every number is the mean
over those $8$ trajectories. The families of Table~\ref{tab:census} were run with
three seeds each. A second batch repeats the lift-free and lifted $\Wu$ families
on the four wave problems and SNO and FNO on the two $1$-D problems, with the
same seeds; runs the lifted and lift-free families on the two further grids; and
adds controls. \emph{Canonical wrapper}: the core
inside a learned pointwise change of coordinates
($q\mathrel{+}=Ap$, $p\mathrel{+}=Bq$, $q\mathrel{+}=Cp$ with symmetric $A,B,C$,
and its inverse), which acts on channels only and is therefore symplectic in
every diagonal $W$; it adds three parameters. \emph{No residual}: the lifted
variant without the residual update. \emph{Persistence}: $x\mapsto x$.
\emph{Untrained}: the lift-free core with its gains fixed at $0.3$.
\emph{One epoch}: the same core trained for a single epoch.
\emph{Perturbed adjoint}: the adjoint's channel-mixing matrix is replaced by
$M+\epsilon\lVert M\rVert\,\Delta/\lVert\Delta\rVert$ for a fixed random $\Delta$,
which is symplectic in no form. At the first test state we also measure the
end-to-end defect (float64 in $1$-D, float32 otherwise) and the eigenvalues of the
one-step Jacobian ($1$-D and $2$-D).

\paragraph{Unseen resolutions.} On \texttt{wave1d\_dir} and \texttt{wave1d\_cgl}
we train on two resolutions ($32$ and $64$ points, or $33$ and $65$ nodes) with
$256$ trajectories each, and test the one-step error on six, four of them unseen
($24$, $48$, $96$, $128$, or $25$, $49$, $97$, $129$), with $3$ seeds.

\subsection{RQ1: Which form does each construction preserve?}
\label{sec:form}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/defect_matrix.png}
\caption{Symplectic defect \eqref{eq:defect} of every construction against
\emph{both} inner products, over $14$ grid configurations in $1$-D, $2$-D and
$3$-D, mean of three independently initialised operators. Every cell uses the
dense Jacobian except the largest $3$-D grid ($17^3$ nodes), which uses the probe
estimator with $m=96$. The green diagonal is the result: each gradient shear is
exact in the form its adjoint was built from and $O(1)$ in the other. The \ckino{}
kernel is $\approx1.4$ in both: volume-preserving, not symplectic.}
\label{fig:defect}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:defect} is the central measurement, and it bears out P1 on
every count.

\textbf{(i)} Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} holds numerically: the matched-form
defect is at most $4.4\times10^{-16}$, independent of resolution and dimension.
\textbf{(ii)} The two variants are \emph{both} exactly symplectic, in different
forms; neither is ``broken''. \textbf{(iii)} The off-diagonal grows with
dimension, as Remark~\ref{rem:spread} predicts: averaged over grids, models and
seeds it is $0.70$ in $1$-D (range $0.44$--$1.02$), $0.96$ in $2$-D
($0.80$--$1.11$) and $1.20$ in $3$-D ($0.97$--$1.35$). \textbf{(iv)} The
\ckino{} kernel is $1.3$--$1.5$ in both forms, the conflation flagged in
Remark~\ref{rem:volume}.

\paragraph{Dense against probe.} Every grid with at most $4096$ degrees of

both are affordable ($65$, $25^2$ and $13^3$ nodes), drawing $40$ independent

error of $40$ repeats, so there is no detectable bias. The probe-to-probe
standard deviation is $3.8$--$4.4\%$, and $95\%$ of single estimates fall within
$9\%$ of the dense value. The spread falls roughly as $m^{-1/2}$, and sharing

$1.3$--$2\times$ smaller than drawing them independently. The one probe-estimated
cell of Figure~\ref{fig:defect} therefore carries about $\pm4\%$ (one standard
deviation) of probe noise on a value near $1.2$, which affects no comparison in
this paper. On the diagonal every probe returns machine zero, so there is no
noise to report.

\paragraph{A grid where neither standard form is right.} On the mapped grid the
exactly matched $W_{\mathrm{kte}}$ brings no consistent benefit. The
\texttt{seq2seq} and lift-free pairs are within $4\%$ of each other for all
three weights and inconclusive. For the lifted recursive pair $W_{\mathrm{kte}}$
is worse than $\Wc$ in all five seeds and indistinguishable from $\Wu$, but
that comparison is dominated by failures: at step $200$ the error exceeds $3$ in
four of five seeds for $W_{\mathrm{kte}}$, in all five for $\Wu$, and in one for
$\Wc$. The mapped grid therefore does not support ``the matched form is best'' in
any of the pairs.

\paragraph{What this establishes.} The preference follows the grid where the
grid has a standard quadrature: toward $\Wu$ on uniform grids ($17$ of $22$
comparisons, none against) and toward $\Wc$ on CGL nodes (two of three, none
against). That is the reversal the matching hypothesis needed to survive, and it
did. It is not universal, since the non-recursive CGL pair is a tie and the mapped
grid shows nothing, and it is limited by a confound.
The weight also defines the quadrature of the kernel integral, so what was
varied is the weighting of the \emph{whole operator}, not the symplectic form
alone. The data therefore support ``use the grid's quadrature weights'' at least
as directly as ``preserve the grid's symplectic form'', and the control that would
separate them (kernel quadrature fixed, adjoint weight varied) is the main
experiment not performed here (\S\ref{sec:limits}). The answer to RQ2 is
therefore: yes where the grid has a standard quadrature, in both directions,
with effects from $1$ to $20\times$; no on the mapped grid; and not yet
attributable to the symplectic form specifically.

\subsection{RQ3: End-to-end symplecticity and the conventional wrapper}
\label{sec:long}

Each long-horizon run trains a model once and rolls it out autoregressively for
$2\times10^{4}$ steps from the first $8$ test initial conditions, on the four wave
problems with three seeds each. With $c=1$ and $\Delta t=0.02$ on $[-1,1)^d$
that is $400$ time units, or $200$ transits of the domain; for
\texttt{wave1d\_dir} ($\Delta t=0.005$, $L=1$) it is $100$ transits. Nothing at
this horizon is visible in the few-hundred-step main matrix. Following
\S\ref{sec:design}, we keep apart four things that a single error number would
conflate: whether the state stays \emph{bounded}; how far it is from the
reference \emph{trajectory}; whether it keeps the right \emph{phase} and
\emph{amplitude}; and whether it conserves \emph{energy}. We call a run bounded
if its relative RMS at step $2\times10^{4}$ is at most $3$. The threshold does
not matter: the largest error among bounded runs is $1.99$ and the smallest
among the rest is $4.68$, so any threshold in between gives the same
classification.

\paragraph{Which part of the wrapper loses boundedness?} Variants between the

all $18$ runs, including the two non-uniform grids. Inside a learned canonical wrapper it
stays bounded ($12$ of $12$). That the wrapped map is symplectic is guaranteed,
since a composition of symplectic maps is symplectic; that it stays bounded is an
experimental result, not a consequence. Behind a pointwise linear lift and
projection, with no residual update, it is

wrapper, in $4$ of $18$ ($0$ of $6$ on the two non-uniform grids). The exception is

separation of symplecticity from capacity: the canonical wrapper adds three
parameters, while the lifted variants have a different width and rank at the same
$25$k budget, so ``symplectic wrapper versus not'' is crossed with the size of
the wrapper.

\paragraph{Approximate symplecticity, and what boundedness tracks.} Exact
symplecticity is not necessary at this horizon either. Perturbing the adjoint's
channel mixing by $1\%$ or $10\%$, which gives a measured end-to-end defect of
$2\times10^{-4}$ to $4\times10^{-3}$, leaves all $12$ runs bounded, with the
error at most $1.4\times$ that of the unperturbed core and the energy drift up to
$8\times$ higher, while a $100\%$ perturbation (defect $2$--$3\times10^{-2}$)
is unbounded in all $6$ (Figure~\ref{fig:stability}b). What boundedness did track
is the spectral radius $\rho$ of the one-step Jacobian at the first test state
(Figure~\ref{fig:stability}a). Among $102$ trained maps on the $1$-D and $2$-D
problems, every one with $\rho\le1+3\times10^{-4}$ was bounded ($59$ of $59$) and
one of $43$ above it was, an SNO run. That is where linear growth would put the
boundary, since $(1+3\times10^{-4})^{2\times10^{4}}\approx e^{6}$. The core and
the canonical wrapper have $\rho$ within $2\times10^{-6}$ of one, with all
eigenvalues on the unit circle; the $1\%$ and $10\%$ perturbed maps sit at
$1.000005$ and $1.00017$ (bounded) and the $100\%$ ones at $1.001$--$1.003$
(unbounded); SNO has a median of $1.0006$, the linear-lift variant $1.0013$,
FNO $1.008$ and the full wrapper $1.014$. A symplectic map need not have
$\rho=1$, as the untrained control shows, but the trained ones did. The
end-to-end defect, which the instrument of \S\ref{sec:defect} measures, separates
the groups less sharply: every map with defect $\le10^{-6}$ was bounded
($36$ of $36$), but $22$ of $65$ above $10^{-3}$ still were, and across the $114$
trained maps its rank correlation with the final error is $0.63$. Over training
epochs its rank correlation with the growth of the state norm, $0.58$, is no
higher than that of the one-step validation error, $0.62$. The defect says
whether a map is symplectic, not whether it will be stable. Two caveats apply:
$\rho$ is evaluated at one state, so it is a local diagnostic rather than a proof,
and the correlations pool different families and problems.

\paragraph{Agreement between the two batches.} The second batch re-ran the
long-horizon runs of the first for the lift-free $\Wu$ and lifted $\Wu$ families on
the four wave problems and for SNO and FNO on the two $1$-D problems, with the
same seeds ($36$ paired runs). The classification into bounded and unbounded agrees in $33$
of them: the matched-form lift-free operator is bounded in all $12$ again, the
lifted $\Wu$ variant in $4$ of $12$ again (with the individual seeds on
\texttt{wave2d} and \texttt{wave3d} changing), and SNO in $4$ of $6$ against $3$ of $6$.

\paragraph{What this means physically.} After $100$--$200$ domain transits the
bounded lift-free model still carries a wave of about the right size, travelling
with the wrong phase, and it has not conserved energy: $|\Delta E/E|$ on its own
trajectory is $1.26$--$6.25$ in the first batch and $1.03$--$6.51$ in the
second, an energy error of $103$--$651\%$, against at most $0.034\%$ for the
reference leap-frog integrator over the same horizon. Since
the amplitude is roughly right, the excess must sit mostly in the gradient term
$c^{2}\lVert\nabla u\rVert^{2}$, consistent with the roughening visible in
Figures~\ref{fig:fields-dir} and~\ref{fig:fields-long}. The best energy
behaviour of any bounded trained run in either round, a lifted $\Wu$ run on
\texttt{wave3d}, is $4.3\%$ (second batch) and $6.8\%$ (first batch), still two orders
of magnitude worse than the reference. The identity map has exactly zero drift,
for the trivial reason that it does not move. No learned operator here conserves
energy in a physically useful sense at this horizon, and we do not claim one
does.

\paragraph{What it does not mean.} Backward-error analysis explains why
symplectic \emph{integrators} conserve a modified Hamiltonian over exponentially
long times \citep{hairer2006gni}, but it assumes a consistent approximation to a
specified flow at small step size, and builds a modified equation from exactly
those hypotheses. A learned operator satisfies none of them merely by having a
symplectic Jacobian: it is not a discretisation of a known Hamiltonian at small
$\Delta t$, and no modified Hamiltonian is available to appeal to. The energy
errors above are what one should expect in the absence of such a guarantee.
\citet{makara2026sno} prove a modified-Hamiltonian stability result for
symplectic learned maps that are smooth Hamiltonian perturbations of the exact
flow, under assumptions on the learned error map (a Hamiltonian perturbation
representation and a controlled BCH expansion); neither hypothesis is checked for
our maps, which at this horizon are far from the exact flow. What we observe is
consistent with the qualitative motivation of geometric integration, and we
report it on that footing; it is not an instance of the theorem.

\begin{remark}[Read the slope with the error]
A drift exponent near zero is \emph{not} by itself evidence of health. FNO scores
$-0.02$ on \texttt{wave1d} while sitting at relative RMS $7\times10^{5}$: once the
state has blown up, the drift ratio saturates and its slope becomes meaningless.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{Related evidence.} \citet{sulskis2026generic} report a qualitatively
similar effect for the metriplectic case: over $200$-step rollouts, the
unconstrained models they test diverge or collapse while their
structure-preserving operator stays bounded, at the cost of accuracy on pure
transport and on their smallest backbone. That constructions enforcing different
structures (symplectic here, GENERIC there) are both reported to stay bounded
where unconstrained models do not suggests the effect is not specific to one
bracket; our \texttt{wave3d} result shows that exact structure alone does not
guarantee it.

\paragraph{The same result seen as fields.} Figures~\ref{fig:fields-dir} and
\ref{fig:fields-long} show the state itself rather than summary curves, for the
first test trajectory of seed $0$, the only seed for which fields were stored.
A single trajectory can mislead in both directions, so each row is labelled with
the family's population result (median error over seeds and the bounded count).
On \texttt{wave2d}, seed $0$ is the seed in which the lifted $\Wu$ variant
diverges; in the other two it stays bounded and is more accurate than the
lift-free model. And SNO's plotted trajectory stays bounded on both problems,
although averaged over the test trajectories it diverges in most seeds. Read

lifted variants and FNO), a bounded oscillation of the wrong shape (SNO's
plotted trajectory on \texttt{wave1d\_dir}), and gradual roughening at roughly

short-horizon, RMS-only evaluation would pass.

\subsection{Repeat execution}
\label{sec:repro}

Operator-learning results are rarely re-run, including by their own authors.
Seed counts of three are common, margins of a few percent are reported as wins,
and there is usually no way for a reader to tell which of those wins would
survive re-running the study; the variance of benchmark comparisons and the
practice of reporting it are discussed by \citet{bouthillier2021variance}, and
reproducibility checklists and programmes by
\citet{pineau2021reproducibility}. Part of our matrix exists in two executions on
the cluster, which lets us check some of our results. The duplication is partial,
so we state exactly what it covers before using it.

\paragraph{What was repeated.} Execution B re-ran $657$ of the $783$ matrix
runs and seed $0$ of each long-horizon problem. The other $126$ matrix runs,
namely the lift-free pair on every problem and the \sacheb{} and SNO
configurations on \texttt{wave2d}, \texttt{wave3d} and \texttt{ns2d}, exist in a
single execution and appear unchanged in both result sets, as do long-horizon
seeds $1$ and $2$. One configuration changed between the executions: on the
$1$-D problems execution A had placed SNO off the parameter budget ($18.5$k
parameters for the recursive variant, $31$k for \texttt{seq2seq}), and execution
B re-matched it to $\approx25$k; every other configuration is identical. Both
executions used the same code, seeds and GPU type, on different hosts. T-FNO,
DeepONet, the transformer and GENERIC-FNO reproduced bit for bit, FNO in $93$ of
$102$ runs, and every re-run long-horizon \sacheb{} and SNO run did too. The
re-run matrix \sacheb{} runs did not: at the same seed they differ from
execution A by a median of $13$--$20\%$ in final test error. We have not
isolated the source, which we presume to be floating-point nondeterminism
amplified by training. Execution B's per-problem winners were re-selected from
its own runs, by the same metric as execution A's (\S\ref{sec:splits}).

The additional runs of \S\ref{sec:extras} are a third, partial check, on a
different software version (PyTorch 2.13 against 2.8). They add seeds $3$ and $4$
for the headline pairs of the matrix, so the lift-free pair, which exists in one
execution of three seeds, has five; and they repeat the long-horizon runs of the
lift-free and lifted $\Wu$ families (and SNO and FNO in $1$-D) on all three seeds. Matrix seeds $3$ and $4$
extend rather than repeat the first three, but they were run independently of
them.

We call this a repeat execution rather than a replication, and the distinction is
worth keeping. The executions share an implementation, an experimental design, a
cluster and a set of authors. The comparison bounds how much of the study is an
artefact of one particular execution. It says nothing about whether an
independent group writing an independent implementation would reach the same
conclusions, which is what replication properly means and is not something
authors can certify for themselves.

\paragraph{What survived.} Every claim that was re-executed survived at the
level at which we make it (Table~\ref{tab:repro}); the per-problem winner on
advection did not. There the contending Chebyshev configuration varies by
$15.7\times$ across seeds in execution B ($0.00062$ to $0.00968$) against SNO's
$1.4\times$. That is not a close race between two good models; it is one
unstable configuration occasionally landing well, and under execution A alone it
would have been reported as a win for our own operator. Below the level of the
claims the noise is larger still: the recursive heat pair of the form ablation
favoured the matched form by $1.58\times$ in execution A and the mismatched form
by $1.02\times$ in execution B, with the same seeds. Which problems count as
clear wins also moved: heat was clear for T-FNO in A and a tie in B, \texttt{kdv} a tie in
A and clear for SNO in B, though our construction's share of the tally did not
change.

\paragraph{What follows.} First, a working rule rather than a statistical test:
with three seeds we treat a margin that falls within the combined seed spread of
the two contenders as inconclusive, not as a result (\S\ref{sec:accuracy}); with
five seeds we apply an interval and an effect floor instead
(\S\ref{sec:extras}). The re-run shows why, since re-running alone moved the
same configuration by $13$--$20\%$. Second, a reporting standard: the seed spread
should be published alongside the mean, because it is what tells a reader whether
a margin is load-bearing, and we report both throughout. Third, the claims marked
\emph{not repeated} in Table~\ref{tab:repro} rest on one execution, and the
winners of the $2$-D and $3$-D problems in Table~\ref{tab:winners} still come
from one.

\subsection{Supporting axes}
\label{sec:support}

\paragraph{Darcy (elliptic, Dirichlet).} The problem is $-\nabla\!\cdot(a\nabla
u)=1$ on $(0,1)^2$ with $u=0$ on the boundary and a smooth log-normal permeability
$a$ drawn from a random field with $8\times8$ modes, solved by a five-point
finite-difference scheme and conjugate gradients, with $512$ training samples at
$64^2$. \ckino{} attains $8.7\times$ lower
relative $L^2$ error than FNO overall and $18.3\times$ lower \emph{at the
boundary}, transferring zero-shot from $64^2$ to $128^2$. This is the clearest
non-periodic win for the Chebyshev basis.

\paragraph{Capacity ceilings.} A 6k--400k sweep shows \ckino{}'s Burgers failure
is \emph{representational}, flat at $\approx0.25$ across a $60\times$ range, and
its recursive instability on \texttt{kdv} persists at every budget. Neither is fixed by scale.

\paragraph{Discretisation invariance.} Training at $N=64$ and evaluating at
$N=128$, \textbf{SNO and T-FNO win this axis outright}: their error changes by
less than $2\%$ between the two grids \emph{and} they are accurate, which is the
combination that matters. \ckino{} with a convolutional lift degrades by
$57\times$ on advection. The spectral lift restores invariance ($0.998$) but
trains unreliably at the training grid: one seed matches the convolutional lift
($1.2\times10^{-3}$) and two fail ($0.59$, $0.84$). There is no setting of our
construction that is both invariant and reliably accurate, and we do not have a
fix. If a workflow requires evaluating at a resolution it did not train on, that
is a decisive argument for the Fourier family and against ours.

\paragraph{Unseen resolutions.} Training on two resolutions and testing the
one-step error on four unseen ones (\S\ref{sec:extras}) separates the bases
at the coarse end only (Figure~\ref{fig:multires}). At the coarsest unseen
resolution ($25$ CGL nodes or $24$ points) the Chebyshev operators' error is
$5$--$19\times$ (CGL) and $11$--$34\times$ (Dirichlet) that of the best Fourier
operator, SNO or FNO. At the finer unseen resolutions all models are within
$1.2$--$1.5\times$ of each other. This is the same verdict as for the
$64\to128$ test above and extends it: the Chebyshev construction does not
transfer to coarser grids, and the Fourier operators do.

\paragraph{Inference speed.} Operators beat the reference solver only where the
solver is expensive: FNO is $3.6\times$ faster on Burgers, $8.9\times$ on \texttt{kdv}
and $5.6\times$ on \texttt{ns2d}; \ckino{} is $2.6\times$ faster on \texttt{kdv} but
$0.07\times$ on advection. These are single wall-clock measurements and moved by
up to $15\%$ between the two executions.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
% ============================================================================

\textbf{The form is not separated from the quadrature rule.} In the
implementation the weight $W$ defines the quadrature of the kernel integral
inside $K$ as well as the adjoint $K^{*}$ (\S\ref{sec:sacheb-arch}), so the
matched and mismatched variants differ in the forward operator too. The
experiments of RQ2 therefore show that using the grid's quadrature weights
throughout helps where the grid has a standard quadrature; they do not show that
the \emph{symplectic form} is what matters. The control that would separate the
two keeps the kernel quadrature fixed and varies only the weight in the adjoint.
It is straightforward to build and has not been run, and until it is the title
question is answered for the operator as a whole rather than for the form alone.

\textbf{The matching hypothesis is only partly supported.} Both directions are
tested, on three $1$-D grids, with five seeds. The preference follows the grid
on uniform grids ($17$ of $22$ comparisons) and on CGL nodes (two of three), but
the non-recursive CGL pair is a tie and the mapped grid, where neither standard
form is the quadrature, shows no benefit from the exactly matched weight. The two
non-uniform grids are $1$-D and Dirichlet, and the reversal is much smaller ($1.4\times$)
than the forward effect ($14$--$21\times$); we have not explained why.

\textbf{The batches differ, and repetition is partial.} The additional runs used
a different software version (PyTorch 2.13 against 2.8) and $32$ test trajectories
per matrix run against $12$, from the same split seeds, and we pool seeds $0$--$2$
with $3$--$4$ only through the per-seed error. $126$ of the $783$ main-matrix
runs, and the mismatched lift-free long-horizon runs, exist in a single execution
(\S\ref{sec:repro}).

\textbf{Winner selection in Table~\ref{tab:winners}.} The best configuration per
problem is chosen on the test split rather than on validation, so those
per-problem numbers are optimistic by an unquantified amount
(\S\ref{sec:splits}). The paired comparisons the argument rests on are not
affected, since no configuration is selected from a pool.

\textbf{The wrapper ablation is confounded with capacity.} The staged ablation of
\S\ref{sec:long} shows that the non-symplectic linear lift and projection, not the
residual update, is where boundedness is lost, but the canonical wrapper adds
three parameters while the lifted variants have a different width and rank at the
same budget. ``Symplectic wrapper versus not'' is therefore crossed with the size
of the wrapper, which we did not vary independently. A canonical wrapper with a
comparable number of parameters would remove the confound.

\textbf{The controls are small and the diagnostics local.} The spectral radius
that tracked boundedness is evaluated at one state, for the $1$-D and $2$-D
problems only, and the correlations of \S\ref{sec:long} pool families and
problems. The identity map shows that boundedness alone is weak evidence, and the
evidence that the bounded symplectic maps keep some dynamics is a factor of three
to four in late-window error over that map.

\textbf{Fields are shown for one seed.} Field snapshots were stored for seed $0$
only, so Figures~\ref{fig:fields-dir} and~\ref{fig:fields-long} show one
trajectory of one seed; the population numbers are in Tables~\ref{tab:census},
\ref{tab:long} and~\ref{tab:staged}.

\textbf{Parameter count is the only matched resource.} Equal parameters does not
mean equal FLOPs, memory or wall-clock across families with arithmetic profiles
as different as these (\S\ref{sec:design}); accuracy comparisons here should not
be read as efficiency comparisons.

\textbf{Re-implementations.} SNO, GENERIC-FNO, FNO, T-FNO, U-FNO, U-Net, DeepONet
and the Transformer are our own implementations from the published descriptions,
not the authors' or library code, and were not tuned to the authors' protocols.
In particular SNO is run here at $25$k to $262$k parameters, whereas its authors
train models of about $2.3$M parameters on $10^{5}$ one-step pairs
\citep{makara2026sno}; our SNO numbers are not a reproduction of theirs.

\textbf{Our GENERIC-FNO implementation.} It diverged in all 12 entries. Parameterising the
PSD multiplier as $M=b^2$ with $b$ initialised at zero places it at a saddle
($\partial M/\partial b=2b=0$), so the dissipative channel never activates. This
is a defect of our implementation, \emph{not} evidence about the published
method, and no conclusion should be drawn from those rows.

\textbf{The \texttt{kdv} problem is linear as generated.} The exponential
integrator that generates \texttt{kdv} multiplies its nonlinear term by an extra
factor of the micro-step, so the stored trajectories are those of the linear
dispersive equation $u_t=u_{xxx}$ (Table~\ref{tab:systems}). Every result labelled
\texttt{kdv} is a result on a linear problem, and the benchmark contains no
nonlinear dispersive case: its nonlinear coverage rests on Burgers and
\texttt{ns2d} alone. A correctly written solver (problem \texttt{kdv\_nl} of the accompanying code)
conserves $\int(u_x^2+2u^3)\,dx$ for $u_t+6uu_x-u_{xxx}=0$ to $4\times10^{-5}$ over
$100$ stored steps, and the benchmark should be re-run with it before any claim
about solitons is made. The Burgers reference is first-order in time (ETD1), at a
micro-step of $\Delta t/8$, and the other reference solvers are as in
Table~\ref{tab:systems}.

\textbf{Canonical structure only.} The lift-free construction needs a canonical
$(q,p)$ pair and is therefore undefined on the scalar-field problems (advection,
heat, Burgers, \texttt{kdv}, \texttt{ns2d}). The Korteweg--de Vries equation is
Hamiltonian only under the non-canonical bracket of \citet{gardner1971kdv}, which
we do not cover.

\textbf{Scale and horizon.} Domains are small ($32$--$64$ points in $1$-D, $32^2$,
$16^3$, $64^2$), adequate to resolve the reported orderings but not a deployment
benchmark. $2\times10^{4}$ steps separates bounded from unbounded but is still far
short of the regimes where backward-error analysis is usually invoked, and the
capacity sweep of Figure~\ref{fig:capacity} covers recursive configurations
only.

\textbf{Single hardware.} All runs of both batches used one accelerator type (Tesla T4)
with TF32 disabled, so arithmetic is consistent across the matrix; results may
differ on accelerators with different defaults.

\textbf{Availability.} Per-run result files, the analysis scripts and the figure
generators are archived with the project code. Model checkpoints and stored field
snapshots (seed $0$ only) are not archived, so the field figures cannot be
regenerated from archived data alone.

\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================================

We set out to test whether geometric structure helps a learned operator and found
that the question, as usually posed, is incomplete. Symplecticity is defined
relative to an inner product; on a non-uniform grid two reasonable
implementations preserve two different forms, both exactly, and only measurement
distinguishes them. Once the form is named the picture is clearer but not
simple. Matching the weights to the grid helps where the grid has a standard
quadrature, in both directions: on uniform grids in $17$ of $22$ comparisons and
by $14$--$21\times$ for the end-to-end symplectic pair, and on CGL nodes in two of
three, by a smaller margin. It brings nothing on a mapped grid where neither
standard form is right, and because the weights also define the kernel's
quadrature, it is not yet attributable to the symplectic form rather than to the
quadrature rule; the control that would settle that is the next experiment.
Symplecticity that holds end to end went with bounded rollouts of $2\times10^{4}$
steps in $60$ of $63$ runs, and a non-symplectic linear lift and projection
lost it in most, but the controls limit what that shows: an identity map is
bounded, an untrained symplectic map is not, a small perturbation of the adjoint
leaves the map bounded, and what boundedness tracked was the spectral radius of
the one-step Jacobian. The bounded maps are not accurate at that horizon, with
errors comparable to the signal and energy off by more than $100\%$. In these
experiments, then, structure preservation buys reliability rather than accuracy,
and the relevant property looks more like near-neutral linear stability than
exact symplecticity. We also show by example that a unit Jacobian determinant is
not preservation of $\omega$, and we think the general lesson is that such claims
should be measured rather than argued: at scale the measurement needs only
standard Jacobian--vector and vector--Jacobian products and does not form the
Jacobian.

\section{A volume-preserving update that is not symplectic}
\label{app:volume}
% ============================================================================

Each St\"ormer--Verlet-style half-step of the \ckino{} block (\S\ref{sec:ckino})
has a Jacobian that is triangular with unit diagonal blocks, hence determinant
$1$. It is tempting to conclude that each half-step preserves $\omega$, but the
step from ``determinant $1$'' to ``preserves $\omega$'' is invalid. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:shear}, $\det D\Phi=1$ holds for every shear, while
preservation of $\omega_W$ additionally requires $WA=A^{\top}W$. The kernel
\eqref{eq:ckino-kernel} of \ckino{} uses independent $\varphi_r,\psi_r$ and an
unconstrained channel mix, so $A$ is not self-adjoint, and the measured defect is
$\approx1.4$ in \emph{both} candidate forms (Figure~\ref{fig:defect}). The same
step bears on the energy behaviour of the block: the backward-error analysis of
symplectic integrators, which gives a modified Hamiltonian conserved over
exponentially long times \citep{hairer2006gni}, requires symplecticity, and volume
preservation alone does not imply the existence of a modified Hamiltonian.

\paragraph{Scoring against a single inner product.} A defect measured against one
form only can mislabel a construction. The uniform-form variant of \sacheb{} has an
$O(1)$ defect when scored against $\Wc$ alone and would be called non-symplectic;
scored against both forms (Figure~\ref{fig:defect}) it is exactly symplectic in
$\Wu$. This is why the defect is always reported against both inner products.

\section{The weighted adjoint in the low-rank basis}
\label{app:lowrank}
% ============================================================================

With $\alpha_r=\iprod{\psi_r}{v}_W$ and $(Kv)(x)=\sum_r\varphi_r(x)M_r\alpha_r$,
\[
\iprod{Kv}{g}_W
=\sum_r (M_r\alpha_r)^{\top}\iprod{\varphi_r}{g}_W
=\sum_r \alpha_r^{\top}\big(M_r^{\top}\iprod{\varphi_r}{g}_W\big)
=\Big\langle v,\;\sum_r \psi_r\,M_r^{\top}\iprod{\varphi_r}{g}_W\Big\rangle_W,
\]
which is exactly $\iprod{v}{K^{*}g}_W$. Hence the weighted adjoint is obtained by
exchanging $\varphi$ and $\psi$ and transposing the channel mixing; no inverse of
$W$ is formed and the cost remains $O(RN^d)$. In $d$ dimensions the weight is the
outer product of the per-axis weights and remains diagonal, so both this identity
and Theorem~\ref{thm:sacheb} carry over unchanged.
